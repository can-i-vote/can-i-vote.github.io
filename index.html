Why we never ask for your name

There is no single list of every voter in America. Each state keeps its own list. No app, no website, and no AI can look you up across the whole country.

If a website says an AI checked your voter record, it is guessing.

A guess is dangerous here. If a website tells you "you are signed up" and it is wrong, you might skip signing up. Then you show up on election day and get turned away. That is a real thing that happens to people.

So we do not guess. Your state has the real list, and your state's website will tell you the truth for free, in about two minutes.

Why can’t I just type it in here, and you do the search?

It is a fair question, and it is the first thing most people ask. There are three reasons, and they are all in your favour.

One: your browser will not allow it. No website is permitted to type into another website’s form. That rule is built into every browser on earth, and it is the single thing that stops fake sites from harvesting what you type on real ones. It is protecting you right now.

Two: it would mean holding your details. To get around that rule we would have to take your name and birthday onto our own computer and pretend to be you on 51 different state websites. We would then be holding exactly the information we promised never to hold — and storing it would make this site worth attacking.

Three: the states block it on purpose. State election sites are built to refuse automated visits, precisely so nobody can bulk-query voter records. When we tested every state link on this site, around twenty of them turned away an automated request. They open perfectly in a normal browser.

So the fastest safe route really is the one in step 2: we tell you exactly what to have ready, then one click takes you straight to the right page. That is about two minutes, and nobody but your own state ever sees your details.

We also do not want your information

Your name, your birthday, and your address together are exactly what a thief needs to open a credit card in your name. Any website that collects all three becomes a target.

So this site collects nothing. No forms. No accounts. No cookies. No tracking. No ads. Nothing about your visit is sent anywhere.

The page does save one thing, on your own device, so you do not have to start again next time: the state you picked. It never leaves your browser, and there is a button to wipe it. See exactly what is stored.

Your state's website will ask for your name and birthday. That is normal and safe — it is the government's own system, and it needs that to find your record.

Where this information comes from

Every link on this site points to a system run by a state election office. We did not write any of the voter rules ourselves.

A few states run their official systems on addresses that do not end in ".gov" — Arizona, Arkansas, Kansas and Washington DC among them. Those are still the real systems. Here is why.

  • State lookup, sign-up, mail ballot, polling place and ID links come from CanIVote.org (opens in a new tab), run by the National Association of Secretaries of State. Collected 1 September 2026.
  • Which states let you vote by mail without a reason comes from the National Conference of State Legislatures (opens in a new tab). Their table was last updated 17 July 2026.
  • The U.S. House district finder is house.gov (opens in a new tab).

Voting rules change. If what you read here does not match your state's official page, believe your state's page, not us.
