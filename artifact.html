Why we never ask for your name

There is no single list of every voter in America. Each state keeps its own list. No app, no website, and no AI can look you up across the whole country.

If a website says an AI checked your voter record, it is guessing.

A guess is dangerous here. If a website tells you "you are signed up" and it is wrong, you might skip signing up. Then you show up on election day and get turned away. That is a real thing that happens to people.

So we do not guess. Your state has the real list, and your state's website will tell you the truth for free, in about two minutes.

Why can’t I just type it in here, and you do the search?

It is a fair question, and it is the first thing most people ask. There are three reasons, and they are all in your favour.

One: your browser will not allow it. No website is permitted to type into another website’s form. That rule is built into every browser on earth, and it is the single thing that stops fake sites from harvesting what you type on real ones. It is protecting you right now.

Two: it would mean holding your details. To get around that rule we would have to take your name and birthday onto our own computer and pretend to be you on 51 different state websites. We would then be holding exactly the information we promised never to hold — and storing it would make this site worth attacking.

Three: the states block it on purpose. State election sites are built to refuse automated visits, precisely so nobody can bulk-query voter records. When we tested every state link on this site, around twenty of them turned away an automated request. They open perfectly in a normal browser.

So the fastest safe route really is the one in step 2: we tell you exactly what to have ready, then one click takes you straight to the right page. That is about two minutes, and nobody but your own state ever sees your details.

We also do not want your information

Your name, your birthday, and your address together are exactly what a thief needs to open a credit card in your name. Any website that collects all three becomes a target.

So this site collects nothing. No forms. No accounts. No cookies. No tracking. No ads. Nothing about your visit is sent anywhere.

The page does save one thing, on your own device, so you do not have to start again next time: the state you picked. It never leaves your browser, and there is a button to wipe it. See exactly what is stored.

Your state's website will ask for your name and birthday. That is normal and safe — it is the government's own system, and it needs that to find your record.

Why we cannot just look you up

People often expect a website to take a name and a birthday and answer "yes, you are registered". It cannot work that way, and here is why.

  • There is no national voter list. The United States has no single database of voters. Each of the 50 states and the District of Columbia runs its own separate list, with its own rules.
  • Those lists are not open to just anyone. Most states restrict who can get voter data and what it can be used for. A website cannot simply query them all.
  • There is no AI that can do it either. An AI can only answer from data it can actually reach. No AI has a live connection to 51 separate state voter systems. If a site claims otherwise, it is guessing at your answer.

A guess is not harmless here. If a site wrongly tells someone they are registered, that person may not sign up, and may be turned away on election day. We would rather send you to the page that gives you a real answer.

What we actually do

  1. You pick your state. That is the only thing you tell us, and it never leaves your browser.
  2. We show you your state's own official links — check your registration, sign up, vote by mail, find your polling place, and what ID you need.
  3. We explain, in plain words, what each one does and what order to do them in.

How to find your district

Your district is the area you vote in. It decides which candidates appear on your ballot. Most people are in several at once: a U.S. House district, a state senate district, a state house district, and a local precinct or ward.

When you look yourself up on your state's official page, it shows your districts right there on the results screen. That is the most accurate source, because it uses your exact address.

The U.S. House also runs a district finder by ZIP code (opens in a new tab). It is quick, but a single ZIP code can cover more than one district, so treat it as a rough answer.

Why the site can’t fill the form in for you

The most common suggestion is: let people type their name and birthday here, and have the site run the search on the state website for them. It cannot be done safely, and mostly it cannot be done at all.

  • Browsers forbid it. The same-origin policy stops any site from reading or writing another site’s form. It is the foundation of web security — the reason a fake page cannot scrape what you type into your bank. There is no way around it from a web page, and there should not be.
  • Doing it server-side means holding the data. The only alternative is to receive name, date of birth and address on our own server and impersonate the user against 51 separate state systems. That recreates the exact identity-theft honeypot this site was designed to avoid, and it would make every claim on the privacy page false.
  • The states actively block it. State voter lookups sit behind bot protection so that nobody can bulk-query voter records — which is a good thing. Health-checking the 255 links on this site produced roughly twenty automated-access refusals from state systems that load fine in a real browser.
  • Terms of use. Several state election sites prohibit automated access outright.

What we can do instead — and what the site now does — is remove every bit of friction around the click: say plainly what to have ready before you start, explain that the state page is safe to type into and why, then hand over one direct link to the exact right page. Two minutes, no account, and no one but your own state government ever sees your details.

Why we don’t look up your district from your ZIP code

It is the obvious shortcut: ask for five digits, show the district, done. We looked at doing exactly that, using the Census Bureau’s own ZIP-code-to-district file. It does not hold up.

  • ZIP codes do not line up with districts. In the Census file, 5,882 of the 33,791 ZIP code areas — about one in six — fall across two or more congressional districts. One spans four. For those people the honest output is "we cannot tell", which is not an answer.
  • ZIP codes are not areas at all. They are postal delivery routes. The Census approximates them with something called a ZCTA, and the two do not always agree.
  • The maps changed. The current Census file dates from October 2024. Since then California, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Texas and Utah have all redrawn their congressional maps for the 2026 elections. A stored list would be confidently wrong for a large share of the country, including its two biggest states — and it would give no hint that it was wrong.

That last point is the one that decided it. A tool that quietly returns a stale district is worse than no tool, because the person believes it. The same reasoning rules out an AI guessing your registration status.

Your state’s lookup uses your exact street address, not a ZIP code, and the state updates it when the maps change. Since you have to visit that page anyway to find out whether you are registered, your districts are already on the screen in front of you when you get there. That is genuinely fewer steps than asking you to type a ZIP code here.

Why some official links are not ".gov"

You have probably been told to trust ".gov" addresses. That is good advice, but a handful of states run their real voter systems on other addresses. For example Arizona uses my.arizona.vote, Kansas uses voteks.org, Arkansas uses voterview.ar-nova.org, Washington DC uses dcboe.org, and New York City uses vote.nyc.

These are the genuine systems. We did not pick them — they are the addresses the state election officials themselves publish on CanIVote.org. If a link here surprises you, you can always check it against CanIVote.org (opens in a new tab) or vote.gov (opens in a new tab) before you type anything in.

Your privacy

This site collects nothing about you. Not your name, not your birthday, not your address, not your visit.

In plain words

  • There are no forms on this site. There is nowhere to type personal information, because we never ask for any.
  • There are no accounts and no logins.
  • There are no cookies and no tracking of any kind.
  • The page does remember which state you picked, on your own device only. It is explained in full below.
  • It also remembers **which language you chose**, in the same way.
  • There is no analytics. We do not count visitors, and we do not know you were here.
  • There are no ads and no advertising pixels.
  • There is nothing sent to any other company. The fonts and the code are stored on this site itself, so your browser never contacts Google, a CDN, or anyone else while the page loads.

Because none of that exists, there is no cookie banner to click and no consent to give. There is nothing to consent to.

The one thing the site remembers

So that you do not have to start again every time, the page saves two short pieces of information on your own device: the state you picked, and whether you said you were signed up or not. When you come back, it takes you straight to where you were.

That is the whole list. For example: {"state":"OH","branch":"no"}. There is no name, no date of birth, no address, and nothing that could identify you.

It is not a cookie, and that matters

A cookie is attached to every request your browser makes to a website, so it travels to whoever runs the server. This uses something different, called local storage, which never leaves your browser. We do not receive it, cannot read it, and could not hand it over if we were asked. That is also why this site has no cookie banner — there is no cookie to ask you about.

How to clear it

There is a button for it. Once you have picked a state, scroll to the bottom of your results and press "Forget the state I picked". If the page greeted you with "Welcome back", there is a "Not you? Start over" button right there too.

It is also cleared whenever you clear your browsing data, and it is never there at all in a private or incognito window.

On a shared or public computer — a library, a school, a friend's laptop — press one of those buttons when you finish, so the next person does not see which state you were looking at.

The web address

When you pick your state, the two-letter code is also added to the address in your browser bar — for example #OH. That is so you can bookmark your state or send the link to someone. It happens inside your browser and is not sent to us. Your browser's own history holds it, exactly as it holds every other page you visit.

Why we designed it this way

Your full name, your date of birth, and your address are, together, the exact set of details someone needs to impersonate you or open credit in your name. A website that collects all three is worth attacking. The safest way to protect that information is to never hold it.

It also means we could not sell, leak, or be forced to hand over your data even if someone asked. There is nothing to hand over.

When you click through to your state

The official state pages we link to are run by state governments, not by us. Once you click one of those links, you are on their website and their privacy policy applies, not ours.

Those state pages will ask for your name and date of birth. That is normal and expected — it is the government's own system, and it is the only way it can find your voter record. It is not the same as an unknown website asking for the same details.

We send no referrer information with those links, so the state site is not told which page you came from.

Hosting

Whoever hosts these files may keep standard server logs, which typically include IP addresses. That is a property of web hosting in general, not something this site sets up or uses. This site's own code stores nothing.

Accessibility

Voting information has to work for everyone. This site is built and tested against WCAG 2.2 Level AA, the standard most commonly used to judge whether a website is accessible.

What we have done

  • Plain language. Short sentences and everyday words. We write "signed up to vote" instead of "registered", and explain jargon like "absentee ballot" the first time it appears.
  • Large text. The body text is 18 pixels, larger than the usual minimum, with generous line spacing.
  • Strong contrast. All text meets or exceeds the 4.5:1 contrast requirement, in both light and dark mode.
  • Full keyboard use. Everything works without a mouse. The focus outline is thick and always visible, and the tab order follows the visual order.
  • Large targets. Every button and link is at least 44 by 44 pixels.
  • Screen reader support. Proper headings, landmarks, and labels. Links that open a new tab say so out loud. Choosing a state or an answer moves focus to the new content and announces it.
  • Never colour alone. The "yes" and "no" choices use words and icons as well as colour.
  • Reduced motion. If your device is set to reduce motion, all movement and smooth scrolling is switched off.
  • Works small and zoomed. Usable down to a 320 pixel wide screen and at 200% zoom, with no sideways scrolling and nothing cut off.
  • Dark mode. Follows your device setting, with contrast checked in both.
  • No time limits, no flashing, no autoplay, and no pop-ups.

What has been tested

  • Automated testing with axe-core against the WCAG 2.0 A, 2.0 AA and 2.2 AA rule sets, with zero violations reported.
  • Manual keyboard testing of the whole page, start to finish.
  • Layout checked at 320, 375, 768, 1024 and 1512 pixels wide, and at 200% zoom.
  • Contrast checked in both light and dark mode.

What has not been tested

We want to be straight with you about the limits.

  • Automated tools catch only a portion of accessibility problems — commonly estimated at around a third. They cannot judge whether wording is clear or whether a page makes sense.
  • This site has not been tested with a real screen reader such as VoiceOver, NVDA or JAWS.
  • It has not been tested by people with disabilities, which is the only test that really counts.

Meeting WCAG 2.2 AA is the goal and the measure used here. It is not a legal guarantee.

If something does not work for you

Tell whoever runs this site and it will be fixed. An accessibility problem on a voting page is a serious bug, not a nice-to-have.

If you need help voting right now and this site is in your way, do not wait for us. Your local election office is required to help you, and can do so by phone. You can find their contact details through CanIVote.org (opens in a new tab).

Your rights

Federal law protects your right to vote with a disability. That includes an accessible voting machine at your polling place, and the right to bring someone of your choice to help you vote. vote.gov explains these rights (opens in a new tab).
