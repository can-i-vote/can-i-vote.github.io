How this works

Short version: we do not have any voter records, and neither does anyone else outside your state government. We show you which official page to use and explain it in plain words.

Why we cannot just look you up

People often expect a website to take a name and a birthday and answer "yes, you are registered". It cannot work that way, and here is why.

  • There is no national voter list. The United States has no single database of voters. Each of the 50 states and the District of Columbia runs its own separate list, with its own rules.
  • Those lists are not open to just anyone. Most states restrict who can get voter data and what it can be used for. A website cannot simply query them all.
  • There is no AI that can do it either. An AI can only answer from data it can actually reach. No AI has a live connection to 51 separate state voter systems. If a site claims otherwise, it is guessing at your answer.

A guess is not harmless here. If a site wrongly tells someone they are registered, that person may not sign up, and may be turned away on election day. We would rather send you to the page that gives you a real answer.

What we actually do

  1. You pick your state. That is the only thing you tell us, and it never leaves your browser.
  2. We show you your state's own official links — check your registration, sign up, vote by mail, find your polling place, and what ID you need.
  3. We explain, in plain words, what each one does and what order to do them in.

How to find your district

Your district is the area you vote in. It decides which candidates appear on your ballot. Most people are in several at once: a U.S. House district, a state senate district, a state house district, and a local precinct or ward.

When you look yourself up on your state's official page, it shows your districts right there on the results screen. That is the most accurate source, because it uses your exact address.

The U.S. House also runs a district finder by ZIP code (opens in a new tab). It is quick, but a single ZIP code can cover more than one district, so treat it as a rough answer.

Why the site can’t fill the form in for you

The most common suggestion is: let people type their name and birthday here, and have the site run the search on the state website for them. It cannot be done safely, and mostly it cannot be done at all.

  • Browsers forbid it. The same-origin policy stops any site from reading or writing another site’s form. It is the foundation of web security — the reason a fake page cannot scrape what you type into your bank. There is no way around it from a web page, and there should not be.
  • Doing it server-side means holding the data. The only alternative is to receive name, date of birth and address on our own server and impersonate the user against 51 separate state systems. That recreates the exact identity-theft honeypot this site was designed to avoid, and it would make every claim on the privacy page false.
  • The states actively block it. State voter lookups sit behind bot protection so that nobody can bulk-query voter records — which is a good thing. Health-checking the 255 links on this site produced roughly twenty automated-access refusals from state systems that load fine in a real browser.
  • Terms of use. Several state election sites prohibit automated access outright.

What we can do instead — and what the site now does — is remove every bit of friction around the click: say plainly what to have ready before you start, explain that the state page is safe to type into and why, then hand over one direct link to the exact right page. Two minutes, no account, and no one but your own state government ever sees your details.

Why we don’t look up your district from your ZIP code

It is the obvious shortcut: ask for five digits, show the district, done. We looked at doing exactly that, using the Census Bureau’s own ZIP-code-to-district file. It does not hold up.

  • ZIP codes do not line up with districts. In the Census file, 5,882 of the 33,791 ZIP code areas — about one in six — fall across two or more congressional districts. One spans four. For those people the honest output is "we cannot tell", which is not an answer.
  • ZIP codes are not areas at all. They are postal delivery routes. The Census approximates them with something called a ZCTA, and the two do not always agree.
  • The maps changed. The current Census file dates from October 2024. Since then California, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Texas and Utah have all redrawn their congressional maps for the 2026 elections. A stored list would be confidently wrong for a large share of the country, including its two biggest states — and it would give no hint that it was wrong.

That last point is the one that decided it. A tool that quietly returns a stale district is worse than no tool, because the person believes it. The same reasoning rules out an AI guessing your registration status.

Your state’s lookup uses your exact street address, not a ZIP code, and the state updates it when the maps change. Since you have to visit that page anyway to find out whether you are registered, your districts are already on the screen in front of you when you get there. That is genuinely fewer steps than asking you to type a ZIP code here.

Why some official links are not ".gov"

You have probably been told to trust ".gov" addresses. That is good advice, but a handful of states run their real voter systems on other addresses. For example Arizona uses my.arizona.vote, Kansas uses voteks.org, Arkansas uses voterview.ar-nova.org, Washington DC uses dcboe.org, and New York City uses vote.nyc.

These are the genuine systems. We did not pick them — they are the addresses the state election officials themselves publish on CanIVote.org. If a link here surprises you, you can always check it against CanIVote.org (opens in a new tab) or vote.gov (opens in a new tab) before you type anything in.

What this site is not

  • It is not a government website and has no connection to any election office.
  • It is not connected to any party, candidate or campaign, and it does not tell anyone how to vote.
  • It is not legal advice. If your situation is complicated — a past conviction, no fixed address, an overseas or military address, a recent move — contact your local election office. They help people with this every day and it is free.
  • It cannot tell you if you are registered. Only your state can.

Found a broken or wrong link?

State election websites move, sometimes without notice. If a link here is broken or sends you somewhere unexpected, go to CanIVote.org (opens in a new tab) directly — it is kept current by the state officials themselves.
